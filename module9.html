01KPIs & Metric Frameworks

A Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is a quantifiable metric used to evaluate success in meeting an objective. Not every metric is a KPI — a KPI must be tied to a strategic goal, measurable, and actionable.

Definition — KPI

A KPI is a metric that directly measures progress toward a specific, prioritized business objective. The distinction between a metric (any measurement) and a KPI (a measurement strategically important to a goal) is crucial in analytics roles.

The SMART Framework for KPIs

Well-defined KPIs satisfy the SMART criteria:

LetterCriterionExample Check
SpecificClearly defined scope — what is being measured and for which segment"7-day active users on mobile" not "activity"
MeasurableQuantifiable with available dataCan pull from DB or analytics tool
AchievableRealistic target given historical baselinesTarget is ±20% of current baseline, not 10×
RelevantConnected to a strategic goalConversion rate → revenue goal
Time-boundMeasured over a defined periodQ3 2024, monthly rolling average

Leading vs. Lagging Indicators

TypeDefinitionExamplesUse
Leading Predicts future performance; changes before the outcome occurs Sales pipeline volume, feature adoption rate, trial-to-paid conversion Proactive course correction; acts as early warning
Lagging Confirms what has already happened; measures outcomes Monthly revenue, customer churn, NPS score Performance evaluation; tells you whether strategy worked
Example — Leading vs. Lagging for SaaS

A SaaS company cares about MRR growth (lagging). Leading indicators could be: number of demos booked, trial-to-paid conversion rate, and feature adoption in the first 7 days. If demos drop in January, MRR will likely drop in February — the leading indicator gives time to react.

Computed Business Metrics (With Formulas)

MetricFormulaInterpretation
Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) Total Sales & Marketing Spend ÷ New Customers Acquired Cost to acquire one new customer. Lower is better; compare to LTV.
Customer Lifetime Value (LTV / CLV) ARPU × Gross Margin % × (1 ÷ Churn Rate) Total expected revenue from one customer. Should be ≥ 3× CAC for healthy unit economics.
LTV:CAC Ratio LTV ÷ CAC Rule of thumb: ≥ 3 is healthy; < 1 means you're losing money per customer.
DAU/MAU Ratio Daily Active Users ÷ Monthly Active Users Stickiness ratio: 0.5+ is excellent (Facebook ~0.65); < 0.1 signals weak engagement.
Churn Rate Customers Lost in Period ÷ Customers at Start of Period Monthly churn of 5% → ~46% annual churn. Even small improvements compound.
Net Revenue Retention (NRR) (Starting MRR + Expansion − Churn − Contraction) ÷ Starting MRR NRR > 100% means revenue grows even without new customers (expansion > churn).
Worked Example — LTV:CAC

Given: ARPU = ₹2,000/month, Gross Margin = 70%, Monthly Churn = 2%, CAC = ₹50,000

LTV = ₹2,000 × 0.70 × (1 ÷ 0.02) = ₹2,000 × 0.70 × 50 = ₹70,000

LTV:CAC = ₹70,000 ÷ ₹50,000 = 1.4

Interpretation: Ratio is below 3, indicating the business is not generating enough value per customer relative to acquisition cost. Need to either lower CAC (optimize marketing), increase ARPU (upsell), or reduce churn.

02Funnel Analysis

A conversion funnel maps the sequential steps users take toward a goal (purchase, signup, activation). Funnel analysis identifies where users drop off, quantifies the drop-off magnitude, and prioritizes optimization efforts.

Definition — Conversion Funnel

A funnel is an ordered sequence of events S₁ → S₂ → … → Sₙ where each stage Sₖ requires completion of all prior stages. The conversion rate from stage i to stage j is: CR(i→j) = Users completing Sⱼ ÷ Users entering Sᵢ.

E-Commerce Funnel — Worked Example

1. Homepage Visit
100,000 users
100.0% overall
↓ 55% drop-off
2. Product View
45,000 users
45.0% — CR: 45%
↓ 40% drop-off
3. Add to Cart
27,000 users
27.0% — CR: 60%
↓ 10% drop-off
4. Checkout Start
24,000 users
24.0% — CR: 89%
↓ 8% drop-off
5. Purchase
16,000 users
16.0% — CR: 67%
Analysis Insight

The biggest absolute drop is Step 1→2 (55,000 users lost), but this may be expected if many visitors are browsing casually. The most actionable opportunity is Step 3→4 (Cart → Checkout), where only 89% proceed — users with intent are abandoning. Cart abandonment emails, guest checkout, and trust signals (secure badge, free returns) could recapture this segment.

SQL Implementation — Funnel Query

SQL
-- Multi-step funnel using conditional aggregation WITH funnel AS ( SELECT user_id, MAX(CASE WHEN event_name = 'page_view' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS step1_homepage, MAX(CASE WHEN event_name = 'product_view' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS step2_product, MAX(CASE WHEN event_name = 'add_to_cart' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS step3_cart, MAX(CASE WHEN event_name = 'checkout_start' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS step4_checkout, MAX(CASE WHEN event_name = 'purchase' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS step5_purchase FROM events WHERE event_date BETWEEN '2024-01-01' AND '2024-01-31' GROUP BY user_id ) SELECT SUM(step1_homepage) AS step1_users, SUM(step2_product) AS step2_users, SUM(step3_cart) AS step3_users, SUM(step4_checkout) AS step4_users, SUM(step5_purchase) AS step5_users, -- Step-over-step conversion rates ROUND(100.0 * SUM(step2_product) / NULLIF(SUM(step1_homepage), 0), 1) AS cr_1_to_2, ROUND(100.0 * SUM(step3_cart) / NULLIF(SUM(step2_product), 0), 1) AS cr_2_to_3, ROUND(100.0 * SUM(step5_purchase) / NULLIF(SUM(step1_homepage), 0), 1) AS overall_cr FROM funnel;

Segmented Funnel Analysis

The aggregate funnel masks important patterns. Always segment by:

  • Acquisition channel: Organic vs. paid vs. referral — each may have very different user intent
  • Device type: Mobile funnels often have lower checkout CR due to UX friction
  • User cohort: New vs. returning users have different buying behavior
  • Geography / language: Trust and payment method preferences vary
Python — Funnel Visualization
import pandas as pd import matplotlib.pyplot as plt stages = ['Homepage', 'Product View', 'Add to Cart', 'Checkout', 'Purchase'] users = [100_000, 45_000, 27_000, 24_000, 16_000] # Conversion rates (step-over-step) cr = [1.0] + [users[i]/users[i-1] for i in range(1, len(users))] fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5)) # Funnel bar chart colors = ['#2B5CE6', '#3d6fe8', '#5480e8', '#6f91e9', '#059669'] axes[0].barh(stages[::-1], users[::-1], color=colors[::-1], edgecolor='none') for i, (u, s) in enumerate(zip(users[::-1], stages[::-1])): axes[0].text(u + 1000, i, f'{u:,}', va='center', fontsize=11) axes[0].set_title('Users at Each Stage', fontweight='bold') axes[0].set_xlabel('Number of Users') # Conversion rate line axes[1].plot(stages, [x*100 for x in cr], 'o-', color='#2B5CE6', lw=2, ms=8) for i, (s, c) in enumerate(zip(stages, cr)): axes[1].annotate(f'{c*100:.0f}%', (s, c*100), textcoords='offset points', xytext=(0,10), ha='center') axes[1].set_title('Step-over-Step Conversion Rate', fontweight='bold') axes[1].set_ylabel('Conversion Rate (%)') axes[1].set_ylim(0, 110) plt.tight_layout() plt.show()

03Cohort Analysis

Definition — Cohort Analysis

A cohort is a group of users who share a common characteristic within a defined time period, typically their acquisition date. Cohort analysis tracks how these groups behave over time, revealing whether product changes improve retention across successive cohorts.

Retention Cohort Table — Interpretation Guide

Rows represent acquisition cohorts (e.g., users acquired in January). Columns represent time since acquisition (Day 0, 7, 14, 30…). Each cell shows the percentage of the cohort still active at that period.

Cohort Day 0 Day 7 Day 14 Day 30 Day 60 Day 90

n=13,800		 100% 52% 41% 31% 23% —

Reading This Table

Column-wise (same period, different cohorts): Day 7 retention improved from 48% → 52% → 55% → 61% across the four cohorts. This shows product improvements are working — newer cohorts retain better at the same time horizon.

Row-wise (same cohort, aging): The Jan cohort drops from 48% at Day 7 to 18% at Day 90. This is the natural retention curve — understand where it flattens (the "plateau") as that represents your loyal core users.

Building a Cohort Table in Python

Python — Cohort Retention Matrix
import pandas as pd import seaborn as sns import matplotlib.pyplot as plt # Assume df has columns: user_id, event_date, acquisition_date df['acquisition_date'] = pd.to_datetime(df['acquisition_date']) df['event_date'] = pd.to_datetime(df['event_date']) # Convert dates to month periods for monthly cohorts df['cohort_month'] = df['acquisition_date'].dt.to_period('M') df['activity_month'] = df['event_date'].dt.to_period('M') df['months_since'] = (df['activity_month'] - df['cohort_month']).apply(lambda x: x.n) # Count distinct active users per cohort × period cohort_data = (df .groupby(['cohort_month', 'months_since'])['user_id'] .nunique() .reset_index() .rename(columns={'user_id': 'active_users'}) ) # Cohort size = users at month 0 cohort_sizes = (cohort_data .query('months_since == 0') .set_index('cohort_month')['active_users'] ) # Pivot and compute retention rates cohort_table = cohort_data.pivot(index='cohort_month', columns='months_since', values='active_users') retention = cohort_table.divide(cohort_sizes, axis=0) * 100 # Heatmap visualization fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(14, 6)) sns.heatmap(retention, annot=True, fmt='.0f', cmap='Blues', linewidths=0.5, ax=ax, cbar_kws={'label': 'Retention %'}) ax.set_title('Monthly Cohort Retention Heatmap', fontsize=14, fontweight='bold') ax.set_xlabel('Months Since Acquisition') ax.set_ylabel('Acquisition Cohort') plt.tight_layout() plt.show()

04RFM Segmentation

Definition — RFM Analysis

RFM is a customer segmentation technique based on three dimensions: Recency (how recently a customer purchased), Frequency (how often they purchase), and Monetary value (how much they spend). Each customer is scored on all three dimensions and grouped into actionable segments.

R

Recency

Days since last purchase. Recent buyers are more likely to purchase again. Score 5 = purchased within the last 7 days; Score 1 = hasn't purchased in 6+ months.

F

Frequency

Number of transactions in the analysis window. High-frequency buyers are habit-formed. Score 5 = 10+ purchases; Score 1 = only 1 purchase (one-time buyer).

M

Monetary

Total revenue from the customer. Identifies high-value customers. Score 5 = top 20% spenders; Score 1 = bottom 20%. Often correlated with Frequency.

Step-by-Step RFM Implementation

1

Define the Analysis Window

Choose a reference date (typically "today" or last day of data). The analysis window (e.g., last 12 months) determines which transactions to include. Recency is measured from this reference date.

2

Calculate R, F, M Values

For each customer: Recency = days since most recent purchase; Frequency = count of orders in the window; Monetary = sum of order values in the window.

3

Score on 1–5 Scale Using Quintiles

Divide customers into 5 equal groups per dimension. Note: for Recency, lower days = higher score (Score 5 = most recent). For F and M, higher values = higher score.

4

Concatenate Scores → Segment

RFM score is the concatenation: R=5, F=5, M=5 → "555" (Champions). Apply business-defined segment labels to score combinations.

5

Design Segment-Specific Actions

Champions → exclusive offers, ambassador programs. At-Risk → win-back campaigns. Promising → onboarding nudges to increase frequency.

Python — Full RFM Implementation
import pandas as pd import numpy as np # Sample transaction data: customer_id, order_date, order_value df['order_date'] = pd.to_datetime(df['order_date']) reference_date = df['order_date'].max() + pd.Timedelta(days=1) # Step 1: Calculate R, F, M values rfm = df.groupby('customer_id').agg( Recency = ('order_date', lambda x: (reference_date - x.max()).days), Frequency= ('order_date', 'count'), Monetary = ('order_value', 'sum') ).reset_index() # Step 2: Score using quintile labels (1–5) # Recency: lower days = better = score 5 rfm['R_score'] = pd.qcut(rfm['Recency'], q=5, labels=[5,4,3,2,1]).astype(int) rfm['F_score'] = pd.qcut(rfm['Frequency'].rank(method='first'), q=5, labels=[1,2,3,4,5]).astype(int) rfm['M_score'] = pd.qcut(rfm['Monetary'], q=5, labels=[1,2,3,4,5]).astype(int) # Step 3: Compute RFM score and segment label rfm['RFM_Score'] = rfm['R_score'].astype(str) + rfm['F_score'].astype(str) + rfm['M_score'].astype(str) def rfm_segment(row): r, f, m = row['R_score'], row['F_score'], row['M_score'] if r >= 4 and f >= 4: return 'Champions' elif r >= 3 and f >= 3: return 'Loyal Customers' elif r >= 4 and f <= 2: return 'Promising' elif r <= 2 and f >= 3 and m >= 3: return 'At Risk' elif r <= 2 and f <= 2: return 'Lost' else: return 'Need Attention' rfm['Segment'] = rfm.apply(rfm_segment, axis=1) # Segment summary summary = rfm.groupby('Segment').agg( Customers = ('customer_id', 'count'), Avg_Recency = ('Recency', 'mean'), Avg_Freq = ('Frequency', 'mean'), Avg_Revenue = ('Monetary', 'mean') ).round(1).sort_values('Avg_Revenue', ascending=False) print(summary)

05Root Cause Analysis

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is the structured process of identifying the fundamental cause of a problem — the "why" behind a symptom — so that fixes address the source rather than the surface manifestation. This is one of the most commonly tested skills in data analyst interviews.

The 5 Whys Technique

Developed by Sakichi Toyoda at Toyota, the 5 Whys iteratively asks "Why?" until the root cause emerges. The number 5 is a guide; it may take fewer or more iterations.

Worked Example — Revenue Drop

Symptom: MRR dropped 18% in March compared to February.

Why 1: Why did MRR drop? → Fewer new subscriptions AND higher churn in March.
Why 2: Why did new subscriptions drop? → Paid ad traffic dropped 40%.
Why 3: Why did paid ad traffic drop? → Campaign budget was cut mid-month.
Why 4: Why was the budget cut? → Finance flagged the ad spend as over-budget in Q1.
Why 5: Why did ad spend go over Q1 budget? → The Q1 marketing budget was set in November before seasonal CPM increases were factored in.

Root Cause: Budget planning process does not account for seasonal advertising cost variations.
Fix: Update budget planning template to include CPM trend analysis; add monthly budget pacing alerts.

Structured Metric Investigation Framework

When asked "Metric X dropped — how would you investigate?", use this structured approach:

1

Confirm the Drop is Real

Check for data pipeline issues: missing data, tracking code changes, logging failures. Verify against multiple data sources. A logging bug can fake a drop. Also check if the drop is within normal variance (seasonality, day-of-week effects).

2

Characterize the Drop

When did it start? (Sudden = likely a specific change or event; gradual = structural trend.) How large is it? Is it improving or worsening? Compare to same period last year and last month.

3

Segment to Isolate

Break down by dimensions: platform (iOS/Android/web), geography, user segment, acquisition channel, product feature. The segment where the drop is concentrated is your lead — a global drop concentrated on Android after an app update points to a specific release.

4

Identify Correlated Changes

What changed around the time the metric dropped? Recent product releases, marketing campaigns, pricing changes, competitor actions, external events (outages, news, seasonal events). Check the changelog and interview stakeholders.

5

Formulate and Test Hypotheses

State hypotheses explicitly: "H1: The drop is caused by the new checkout flow released on March 15." Run the funnel for before/after periods to test. Quantify the impact: "The checkout drop from 89% → 72% explains 85% of the revenue decline."

6

Recommend Action

Separate short-term fixes (rollback the change, hotfix) from long-term root cause solutions (add monitoring, improve QA process). Quantify the expected recovery and timeline.

Complex metrics can be decomposed into constituent parts. This forces a structured look at which component is driving the change.

06MECE & Issue Trees

Definition — MECE

MECE stands for Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. A set of categories is MECE if: (1) Mutually Exclusive — no overlap between categories, and (2) Collectively Exhaustive — all categories together cover 100% of the problem space. MECE is the foundation of structured problem-solving at consulting firms (McKinsey, BCG, Bain) and is equally critical in business analytics.

MECE Test — Good vs. Bad Examples

SegmentationMutually Exclusive?Collectively Exhaustive?MECE?
Users: Mobile | Desktop | Tablet ✓ A user session is one device ✓ All device types covered ✓ MECE
Users: Young | Old | Active ✗ "Active" overlaps with Young and Old ✓ Covers most users ✗ Not MECE
Revenue: Subscription | Advertising ✓ Different revenue streams ✗ Misses transaction fees, licensing, etc. ✗ Not MECE
Costs: Fixed | Variable ✓ All costs are either fixed or variable ✓ No cost falls outside both categories ✓ MECE

Issue Tree — Structured Problem Decomposition

An issue tree is a hierarchical MECE breakdown of a problem into its components. Each level should be MECE. The tree guides investigation: once you identify which branch holds the problem, all other branches can be deprioritized.

Why did DAU drop 20%?

Interview Tip

When solving a case in an interview, always structure your answer using MECE issue trees. Start by naming the branches out loud ("I'll split this into supply-side and demand-side factors…"), then say which branch you'll investigate first and why. This signals structured thinking — the key skill being assessed.

07Case Study Frameworks

Business analytics interviews increasingly include open-ended case questions: "How would you measure the success of Feature X?" or "Revenue is down — walk me through your analysis." Frameworks give structure to your answer.

The Goal → Metric → Dimensions Framework

For any "how would you measure success?" question:

1

Clarify the Goal

What is the business objective? Increase revenue? Improve engagement? Reduce cost? Get explicit about the "why" before choosing metrics.

2

Identify Primary and Guardrail Metrics

Primary metric: the KPI that directly measures the goal. Guardrail metric: a metric that must not degrade while optimizing the primary. Example: primary = conversion rate; guardrail = revenue per session (don't want cheap conversions of low-value carts).

3

Define the User Journey

Map the funnel the feature affects. Where does the feature touch the user journey? What's the before/after state? This reveals which funnel stages to monitor.

4

Decide on Segmentation and Time Horizon

Which user segments are expected to benefit most? What time window is appropriate to see the effect? (Engagement features show quickly; retention features may need 30–90 days.)

5

Propose Measurement Method

A/B test? Pre-post comparison? Holdout group? Quantify sample size and duration needed. Address potential confounders.

Common Case Types and Angles

Metric Drop

Metric X Dropped — What Do You Do?

Use the 6-step RCA framework: confirm data validity → characterize drop → segment → identify changes → test hypotheses → recommend fix. Always start with "first, I'd validate the data."

Feature Launch

How Would You Measure Feature X?

Goal → Primary Metric → Guardrail Metrics → User Journey → Segmentation → A/B test design. Name both adoption metrics (feature usage) and impact metrics (does it move the needle on the business goal?).

Trade-off

Should We Launch Feature X?

Quantify the expected upside, then the risks. Use expected value framework: P(success) × upside + P(failure) × downside. Consider cannibalization, technical debt, opportunity cost.

Estimation

Estimate Market Size / Usage Volume

Fermi estimation: break the problem into estimable components. Population → Addressable segment → Penetration rate → Frequency → Value per instance. State assumptions explicitly and sanity-check the final number.

Fermi Estimation — Worked Example

Question: Estimate the number of Uber rides taken in Mumbai per day.

Population of Mumbai: ~20 million

Smartphone penetration (proxy for app eligibility): ~60% → 12 million smartphone users

Proportion who use ride-hailing (Uber / Ola): ~15% → 1.8 million potential riders

Average ride frequency: ~3 rides per week per active user → 3/7 ≈ 0.43 rides/day

Uber's market share: ~40% of ride-hailing market

Estimate: 1.8M × 0.43 × 0.40 ≈ 310,000 Uber rides/day

Sanity check: Uber typically has ~5–10M rides/day globally across 70+ cities. Mumbai being one of their top cities at 300K seems reasonable. Adjust multiplier if the interviewer says to challenge a specific assumption.

SCQA Framework — Structuring Your Answer

Use SCQA (Situation → Complication → Question → Answer) to open any case presentation. It aligns the listener before diving into analysis.

08North Star Metric

Definition — North Star Metric

A North Star Metric (NSM) is the single metric that best captures the core value a product delivers to customers. It represents the intersection of what customers find valuable and what drives sustainable business growth. It acts as an alignment mechanism — all teams optimizing for the NSM are, by definition, contributing to the product's core purpose.

Properties of a Good North Star Metric

  • Reflects customer value: Measures actual value delivered, not just business activity
  • Leads revenue: When it grows, revenue grows (correlation with long-term business health)
  • Measurable and understandable: Every team member can understand and compute it
  • Actionable: Teams have levers they can pull to influence it
  • Not a vanity metric: Downloads or registered users are not NSMs — they don't capture value delivered

North Star Metrics Across Companies

CompanyNorth Star MetricWhy This?
AirbnbNights bookedA booked night = host earns, guest travels = core value delivered
SpotifyTime spent listeningMore listening = more value consumed → higher retention → more subscription revenue
FacebookDaily Active Users (DAU)Daily engagement drives ad revenue; DAU is a leading indicator of ad inventory
SlackMessages sent per weekTeams sending messages are actively using Slack and less likely to churn
WhatsAppMessages sentEach message = communication value delivered; volume = network health
NetflixHours watched per subscriberWatching = getting value; high watch time → lower churn

UberTrips per dayEach trip = marketplace transaction = revenue + supply/demand health signal

The NSM Equation

The NSM can be decomposed into input metrics (sometimes called "One Metric That Matters" inputs), which gives teams specific levers to pull:

Example — Airbnb NSM Decomposition
Nights Booked =
(Travelers searching) × (Search-to-booking rate) × (Average nights per booking)

Each input is owned by a different team: Growth (travelers searching), Product (search-to-booking), CX (repeat bookings)
Warning — Goodhart's Law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." (Goodhart's Law). If teams optimize for the NSM at all costs, they may game it in ways that harm business health. Always pair the NSM with guardrail metrics: for Airbnb, nights booked should not come at the cost of declining host or guest satisfaction (measured by NPS or review scores).

09Interview Q&A

You notice that DAU has dropped 20% week-over-week. How would you investigate this?

I'd use a structured, step-by-step approach:

Step 1 — Validate the data. Before sounding any alarms, I'd check whether the drop is real. I'd look for: missing data in the pipeline (incomplete logging, ETL failures), any recent changes to the tracking code or event schema, and whether the drop affects all platforms equally. If Android DAU dropped but iOS is stable, it's likely a platform-specific issue, not a product-wide problem.

Step 2 — Characterize the drop. When exactly did it start? Sudden drops (overnight or on a specific day) point to a specific change or incident. Gradual declines over weeks suggest a structural trend. I'd plot DAU over the last 90 days and mark any product releases, campaigns, or external events.

Step 3 — Segment to isolate. I'd break DAU down by: platform (iOS/Android/web), geography, user cohort (new vs. returning), acquisition channel, and device type. The segment where the drop is concentrated gives us our lead hypothesis. If it's concentrated in new users, we look at acquisition or onboarding. If it's returning users, we look at retention and engagement.

Step 4 — Correlate with changes. What changed around the time of the drop? I'd review the product changelog, marketing calendar, and infrastructure incident log. A new app version, a notification system change, or a marketing campaign pause are all common culprits.

Step 5 — Formulate and test hypotheses. I'd state 2–3 hypotheses explicitly and quantify each. Example: "H1: The drop is caused by a notification bug reducing re-engagement. Evidence: push notification delivery rate dropped from 85% to 40% starting March 12." I'd then test each hypothesis with data.

Step 6 — Recommend action. Separate the immediate fix (rollback, hotfix) from the long-term root cause (better QA process, more comprehensive monitoring).

What is a North Star Metric? How would you define one for a food delivery app like Swiggy?

A North Star Metric is the single metric that best captures the core value the product delivers to its users. It sits at the intersection of customer value and long-term business health — when it grows sustainably, revenue and retention tend to follow. It also serves as an alignment mechanism: all teams optimizing for the NSM are, by definition, moving the product toward its core purpose.

A good NSM must be: (1) reflective of customer value delivered, (2) a leading indicator of revenue (not just a lagging financial number), (3) understandable by every team, and (4) actionable — teams must have levers to influence it.

For a food delivery app like Swiggy:

The core value proposition is "the right food delivered quickly and reliably to a customer who's hungry." The best candidate NSM is Orders Delivered per Week (or "successful deliveries per week").

Why not alternatives?

  • App downloads — vanity metric; doesn't measure value delivered
  • GMV — business metric, not a customer value metric; can increase via price inflation without more orders
  • Revenue — lagging; teams can't directly act on it

Decomposition: Orders Delivered = Unique ordering customers × Orders per customer per week. This splits neatly between Growth (acquire new customers) and Product/Retention (increase order frequency per customer). Guardrail metrics would include order completion rate, average delivery time, and customer satisfaction score — we don't want to grow orders by compromising quality.

Explain RFM analysis. How would you use it to prioritize a marketing campaign?

RFM stands for Recency, Frequency, and Monetary — three dimensions that together capture the health and value of a customer relationship:

  • Recency: How recently did the customer last purchase? Recent purchasers are more responsive to marketing.
  • Frequency: How many times have they purchased? Frequent buyers are more loyal and habit-formed.
  • Monetary: How much have they spent in total? High-value customers deserve more investment to retain.

Each customer is scored 1–5 on each dimension (using quintiles), yielding a 3-digit score like "555" (best) or "111" (worst). These scores map to segment labels: Champions (555), Loyal Customers (~4-4-x), At Risk (~2-4-4), Promising (~4-1-1), Lost (~1-1-1), etc.

Campaign prioritization: Rather than sending the same email to all customers, RFM lets you tailor the message:

  • Champions: Exclusive loyalty rewards, early access, ambassador programs. Cost-justified because CLV is high.
  • At Risk: Win-back campaign: "We miss you — 20% off your next order." High ROI if successful; these were loyal customers worth recovering.
  • Promising: Second-purchase nudges, free shipping on next order. Goal is to convert one-time buyers into habit-formed repeat customers.
  • Lost: Minimal investment — cost-effective reactivation email. If no response, sunset communication to preserve deliverability.

The key insight is that marketing ROI is maximized by spending more on segments with high probability × high value, rather than broadcasting the same campaign to everyone.

What does MECE mean and why is it important in analytics?

MECE stands for Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. A MECE breakdown divides a problem space into categories where:

  • Mutually Exclusive: No overlap between categories — each observation belongs to exactly one category
  • Collectively Exhaustive: All categories together cover the entire problem space — nothing falls outside the categorization

Why it matters in analytics:

First, it ensures complete coverage. When investigating a metric drop, if your issue tree isn't collectively exhaustive, you might miss entire categories of root causes and reach a false conclusion.

Second, mutual exclusivity prevents double-counting. In a funnel analysis, if user segments overlap, your "total" will be inflated. In financial analysis, if cost categories overlap, your cost structure is wrong.

Third, it enables clean attribution. If an e-commerce revenue decline is split MECE into (new customers) + (returning customers), you can attribute 100% of the change to these two groups and understand the contribution of each without ambiguity.

Practical test: For any segmentation you create, ask: "Could a single observation legitimately belong to more than one category?" (violates ME) and "Could any observation fall outside all categories?" (violates CE). If the answer to either is yes, redesign the segmentation.

Example: Segmenting users by "age < 25" and "age < 35" violates ME (18-24 year olds appear in both). Fix: "age 18–24" | "age 25–34" | "age 35+" is MECE.

Walk me through a cohort analysis and explain what insights it can reveal that aggregate metrics cannot.

A cohort analysis groups users by a shared characteristic — most commonly their acquisition date — and tracks their behavior over time. The result is a retention matrix where rows are cohorts and columns are time periods since acquisition (Day 7, Day 30, etc.), with each cell showing the percentage of the cohort still active.

Why aggregate metrics hide the truth:

Consider this scenario: your total DAU has been flat at 1 million for 6 months. This sounds stable. But cohort analysis might reveal that each new cohort retains progressively worse — say, 60-day retention has dropped from 40% → 25% over 6 months — and the flat DAU is maintained only because you've ramped up acquisition spending 3×. You're filling a leaky bucket faster, not fixing the leak. A flat aggregate metric masks a worsening retention problem that will eventually become unsustainable when acquisition costs rise or the budget is cut.

Insights cohort analysis reveals:

  • Product improvements working? If Day-30 retention improves from 20% → 30% across successive cohorts, the product changes are sticking.
  • Seasonal quality differences: Holiday-acquired cohorts often retain worse because they're deal-seekers, not core users. Cohort analysis reveals this quality difference invisible in aggregates.
  • Retention curve shape: Where does the retention curve flatten? The plateau level is your "loyal core" — users who will stay indefinitely. A flat-at-30% curve is a different business than one that flat-at-5%.
  • Impact of marketing channels: Cohorts acquired via organic search often retain much better than paid cohorts — cohort analysis by acquisition channel quantifies this trade-off.

The key value of cohort analysis is that it makes time-based comparisons fair — you're always comparing cohorts at the same stage of their lifecycle, not mixing new users (who haven't had time to churn) with veterans.
