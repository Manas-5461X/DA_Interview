1. Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics summarize and describe the features of a dataset. They are the first thing any analyst computes when encountering new data. Understanding their properties deeply — not just their formulas — is what separates strong candidates from weak ones.

1.1 Measures of Central Tendency

Mean (Arithmetic Mean)

The sum of all values divided by the count. The most commonly used measure of center. Sensitive to outliers.

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n = Σxᵢ / n

Median

The middle value when data is sorted. If n is even, average the two middle values. Robust to outliers — preferred for skewed distributions (e.g., income, house prices).

Middle value of sorted dataset

Mode

The most frequently occurring value. A dataset can be unimodal, bimodal, or multimodal. The only valid measure of central tendency for nominal data.

Value(s) with highest frequency

Geometric Mean

The nth root of the product of n values. Used for growth rates, ratios, and multiplicative quantities. Always ≤ arithmetic mean.

GM = (x₁ × x₂ × … × xₙ)^(1/n)
Worked Example — Mean vs. Median

Dataset: Employee salaries (in ₹ thousands/month): [30, 32, 35, 35, 38, 40, 42, 250]

Mean: (30+32+35+35+38+40+42+250) / 8 = 502 / 8 = ₹62.75K

Median: Sort → [30,32,35,35,38,40,42,250]. n=8 (even) → average of 4th and 5th values = (35+38)/2 = ₹36.5K

The CEO earns ₹250K, which inflates the mean to ₹62.75K — a value that misrepresents the typical employee's salary. The median of ₹36.5K is a much more meaningful summary. This is why median income is reported rather than mean income in economic statistics.

1.2 Measures of Spread (Variability)

Range

Maximum − Minimum. Simplest measure of spread but ignores all intermediate values and is heavily affected by outliers.

Range = x_max − x_min

Variance

Average squared deviation from the mean. Population variance uses N; sample variance uses (n−1) to correct for bias (Bessel's correction). Units are squared.

s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n−1)

Standard Deviation (SD)

Square root of variance — returns to original units. The most interpretable measure of spread. About 68% of normally distributed data falls within ±1 SD of the mean.

s = √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n−1)]

IQR (Interquartile Range)

Q3 − Q1. The spread of the middle 50% of the data. Robust to outliers. Used to define the "fence" for outlier detection: [Q1 − 1.5×IQR, Q3 + 1.5×IQR].

IQR = Q3 − Q1
Worked Example — Variance and SD

Dataset: [4, 7, 13, 16]    n = 4

Step 1 — Mean: x̄ = (4+7+13+16)/4 = 40/4 = 10

Step 2 — Squared deviations:

  • (4−10)² = (−6)² = 36
  • (7−10)² = (−3)² = 9
  • (13−10)² = 3² = 9
  • (16−10)² = 6² = 36

Step 3 — Sample Variance: s² = (36+9+9+36) / (4−1) = 90/3 = 30

Step 4 — Sample SD: s = √30 ≈ 5.48

1.3 Shape of a Distribution

Skewness

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution around its mean.

  • Positive skew (right-skewed): The tail extends to the right. Mean > Median > Mode. Common in income distributions, house prices, claim amounts.
  • Negative skew (left-skewed): The tail extends to the left. Mean < Median < Mode. Common in exam scores when most students do well but a few score very low.
  • Zero skew: Symmetric distribution. Mean = Median = Mode (exactly, for a perfect normal distribution).

Kurtosis

Kurtosis measures the "tailedness" — how much of the distribution's variance is due to extreme values.

  • Mesokurtic (kurtosis = 3, excess kurtosis = 0): Normal distribution — the reference.
  • Leptokurtic (excess kurtosis > 0): Heavy tails, sharp peak. Extreme values occur more often than in a normal distribution. Common in financial returns.
  • Platykurtic (excess kurtosis < 0): Light tails, flat peak. Extreme values occur less often than normal.

1.4 Percentiles and Quartiles

The pth percentile is the value below which p% of the data falls. Key percentiles:

  • Q1 (25th percentile): Lower quartile — 25% of data falls below this value.
  • Q2 (50th percentile): Median — 50% of data falls below.
  • Q3 (75th percentile): Upper quartile — 75% of data falls below.
  • P90, P95, P99: Commonly used in system performance analysis (latency, response times) to characterize tail behaviour.
Worked Example — IQR and Outlier Detection

API response times (ms): [120, 135, 140, 145, 150, 155, 160, 162, 165, 900]

Q1 = 140 ms, Q3 = 162 ms   →   IQR = 162 − 140 = 22 ms

Lower fence: Q1 − 1.5 × IQR = 140 − 33 = 107 ms

Upper fence: Q3 + 1.5 × IQR = 162 + 33 = 195 ms

The 900ms response time exceeds the upper fence → it is flagged as an outlier. The IQR method correctly identifies this extreme value even though the other 9 observations are reasonably clustered.

2. Probability Theory

Definition

Probability is a numerical measure of the likelihood that an event will occur, expressed as a number between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). If P(A) = 0.7, then event A is expected to occur in 70% of repeated experiments under identical conditions.

2.1 Basic Probability Rules

RuleFormulaCondition
ComplementP(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)Always
Addition (mutually exclusive)P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)A and B cannot both occur
Addition (general)P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)A and B may overlap
Multiplication (independent)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)A and B do not influence each other
Multiplication (general)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A)A and B may be dependent
Conditional ProbabilityP(B|A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A)P(A) > 0

2.2 Conditional Probability

Definition

Conditional probability P(B|A) is the probability that event B occurs given that event A has already occurred. It narrows the sample space from all outcomes to only those outcomes in which A occurred.

Worked Example — Conditional Probability

A company has 200 employees: 120 are engineers, 80 are in sales. Of the engineers, 36 are senior-level. Of the sales team, 20 are senior-level.

Q: What is the probability that a randomly selected employee is senior-level, given that they are an engineer?

P(Senior | Engineer) = P(Senior ∩ Engineer) / P(Engineer) = (36/200) / (120/200) = 36/120 = 0.30 (30%)

Of all engineers, 30% are senior. Compare to the overall senior rate: (36+20)/200 = 28%. Engineering has a slightly higher senior rate than the company average.

2.3 Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem

P(A|B) = [P(B|A) × P(A)] / P(B)

Where: P(A) is the prior (our belief about A before observing B), P(B|A) is the likelihood (probability of observing B if A is true), P(B) is the marginal likelihood, and P(A|B) is the posterior (updated belief after observing B).

Bayes' theorem formalizes how we update our beliefs in light of new evidence. It is the theoretical foundation of Bayesian statistics, spam filters, medical diagnostic tests, and many ML algorithms.

Worked Example — Bayes' Theorem (Medical Test)

A disease affects 1% of the population. A test for this disease is 99% sensitive (correctly identifies 99% of sick patients) and 95% specific (correctly identifies 95% of healthy patients, i.e., has a 5% false-positive rate).

Q: If a patient tests positive, what is the probability they actually have the disease?

Let D = "has disease", T+ = "tests positive".

  • P(D) = 0.01 (prior: disease prevalence)
  • P(T+|D) = 0.99 (sensitivity)
  • P(T+|Dᶜ) = 0.05 (false-positive rate)

P(T+) = P(T+|D)×P(D) + P(T+|Dᶜ)×P(Dᶜ)
= 0.99 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594

P(D|T+) = P(T+|D) × P(D) / P(T+)
= (0.99 × 0.01) / 0.0594 = 0.0099 / 0.0594 ≈ 16.7%

Interpretation: Despite a positive test result from a highly accurate test, there is only a ~17% chance the patient actually has the disease. This is because the disease is rare (1% prior), so most positives are false alarms. This result, which surprises many people, illustrates the base rate fallacy and the crucial importance of prevalence in interpreting diagnostic tests.

3. Probability Distributions

A probability distribution describes how likely each possible value of a random variable is. Knowing which distribution governs a dataset tells you which statistical methods apply and how to interpret results.

3.1 Discrete Distributions

Used when the variable can take a countable set of values (usually integers).

Bernoulli

Models a single trial with two outcomes: success (probability p) or failure (probability 1−p).

Parameters: p

Mean: p    Variance: p(1−p)

Use: coin flip, click/no-click, purchase/no-purchase

Binomial

Number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials.

Parameters: n, p

PMF: P(X=k) = C(n,k) × pᵏ × (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ

Mean: np    Variance: np(1−p)

Use: number of defective items in a batch, email click-throughs

Poisson

Number of events in a fixed time/space interval, given a constant average rate.

Parameters: λ (average rate)

PMF: P(X=k) = (e⁻λ × λᵏ) / k!

Mean = Variance = λ

Use: website requests per minute, calls to a call centre per hour, rare disease cases per year
Worked Example — Binomial

A sales team closes 30% of leads. If 10 leads are contacted this week, what is the probability of closing exactly 4?

X ~ Binomial(n=10, p=0.3)

P(X=4) = C(10,4) × 0.3⁴ × 0.7⁶ = 210 × 0.0081 × 0.117649 = 210 × 0.0009529 ≈ 0.2001 (20.0%)

Expected closures: E[X] = np = 10 × 0.3 = 3 per week

Worked Example — Poisson

A customer support system receives an average of 8 tickets per hour. What is the probability of receiving exactly 5 tickets in a given hour?

X ~ Poisson(λ=8)

P(X=5) = (e⁻⁸ × 8⁵) / 5! = (0.000335 × 32768) / 120 = 10.976 / 120 ≈ 0.0916 (9.16%)

3.2 Continuous Distributions

Uniform

Every value in [a, b] is equally likely. Used as a reference or baseline distribution.

Mean: (a+b)/2    Variance: (b−a)²/12

Use: random number generation, modelling complete uncertainty over a range

Normal (Gaussian)

Bell-shaped, symmetric, defined by μ and σ. The most important distribution in statistics due to the Central Limit Theorem.

68-95-99.7 Rule: 68% within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ

Use: heights, measurement errors, test scores, many ML model assumptions

Exponential

Time between events in a Poisson process. Memoryless property: P(X>s+t|X>s) = P(X>t).

Parameters: λ (rate)    Mean: 1/λ

Use: time between customer arrivals, machine lifetime, time-to-failure

t-Distribution

Like the normal but with heavier tails; shape depends on degrees of freedom (df). As df→∞, t → standard normal. Used when σ is unknown and sample size is small.

Use: t-tests, confidence intervals for small samples

3.3 The Standard Normal and Z-Scores

Z-Score Formula

z = (x − μ) / σ

A Z-score measures how many standard deviations a value x is from the mean μ. It transforms any normally distributed variable into a standard normal distribution (mean=0, SD=1), enabling probability lookup in standard tables.

Worked Example — Z-Score

Exam scores are normally distributed with μ = 70 and σ = 10. What percentage of students scored above 85?

Step 1: z = (85 − 70) / 10 = 1.5

Step 2: From the Z-table: P(Z ≤ 1.5) = 0.9332

Step 3: P(X > 85) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668 (6.68%)

About 6.68% of students scored above 85.

4. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Central Limit Theorem

If we take sufficiently large random samples (n ≥ 30 is a common rule of thumb) from any population with finite mean μ and finite variance σ², the distribution of the sample mean x̄ will be approximately normally distributed with mean μ and standard error σ/√n — regardless of the shape of the underlying population distribution.

The CLT is the reason that so many statistical tests assume normality even when working with non-normal data: as long as the sample is large enough, the distribution of the sample statistic (not the raw data) will be approximately normal.

Implications of the CLT

  • The standard error (SE = σ/√n) tells us how much sample means vary across repeated sampling. As n increases, SE decreases — larger samples give more precise estimates of the population mean.
  • We can use normal-distribution probability calculations even when the underlying data is skewed, bimodal, or otherwise non-normal.
  • This underpins the validity of t-tests, z-tests, confidence intervals, and many other procedures.
Worked Example — CLT

Daily app session duration follows a right-skewed distribution (many short sessions, a few very long ones) with population mean μ = 12 minutes and σ = 8 minutes. If we sample n = 64 users, what is the probability that the sample mean session duration exceeds 14 minutes?

Standard Error: SE = σ/√n = 8/√64 = 8/8 = 1 minute

By the CLT, x̄ ~ Normal(μ=12, SE=1)

Z-score: z = (14 − 12) / 1 = 2.0

P(x̄ > 14): 1 − P(Z ≤ 2.0) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228 (2.28%)

There is only a 2.28% chance that a random sample of 64 users has a mean session above 14 minutes, even though the distribution is right-skewed. The CLT makes this calculation valid despite the skewness of the underlying data.

5. Confidence Intervals & Statistical Inference

Definition — Confidence Interval

A confidence interval (CI) is a range of values, derived from sample data, that is expected to contain the true population parameter with a specified probability (the confidence level, e.g., 95%). A 95% CI means: if we repeated the experiment 100 times and built a CI each time, approximately 95 of those intervals would contain the true parameter.

Common Misconception

A 95% CI does NOT mean "there is a 95% probability that the true parameter lies in this specific interval." Once the interval is computed, the true parameter either is or is not in it — it's not a probability statement about this specific interval. The 95% refers to the long-run behavior of the procedure across many repetitions.

CI Formula for a Population Mean

Confidence Interval (known σ, large n)
CI = x̄ ± z* × (σ / √n)
Where z* is the critical value from the standard normal distribution corresponding to the desired confidence level: z* = 1.645 for 90%, z* = 1.96 for 95%, z* = 2.576 for 99%.
Confidence Interval (unknown σ, small n)
CI = x̄ ± t* × (s / √n)
Where t* is the critical value from the t-distribution with (n−1) degrees of freedom. Use this when σ is unknown and n < 30.
Worked Example — Confidence Interval

A random sample of 49 customer transactions has a mean value of ₹1,250 and a sample standard deviation of ₹350. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the true mean transaction value.

Given: x̄ = 1250, s = 350, n = 49, confidence level = 95%

Standard Error: SE = s/√n = 350/√49 = 350/7 = 50

Critical value: For 95% CI with n=49 (use t-distribution, df=48), t* ≈ 2.01 (from t-table)

Margin of Error: ME = t* × SE = 2.01 × 50 = ₹100.5

95% CI: [1250 − 100.5, 1250 + 100.5] = [₹1,149.5, ₹1,350.5]

Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true mean transaction value lies between ₹1,149.5 and ₹1,350.5.

6. Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing is a formal procedure for using sample data to evaluate a claim about a population parameter. It is one of the most commonly tested topics in data analytics interviews.

6.1 The Framework

  1. State H₀ (Null Hypothesis): The default assumption — "nothing is happening," "no effect," "no difference." H₀ always contains an equality (=, ≤, ≥).
  2. State H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): The claim we are testing for — "there is an effect," "there is a difference." H₁ contains an inequality (<, >, ≠).
  3. Choose significance level α: Common choices are 0.05 (5%) or 0.01 (1%). This is the probability of incorrectly rejecting H₀ that we're willing to accept.
  4. Collect data and compute test statistic.
  5. Compute p-value: The probability of observing data at least as extreme as what we observed, assuming H₀ is true.
  6. Decision: If p-value ≤ α, reject H₀. If p-value > α, fail to reject H₀.

6.2 The p-Value — In Depth

Definition

The p-value is the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme as (or more extreme than) the one computed from the sample, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value means the observed data would be very unlikely under H₀, providing evidence against H₀.

What the p-value is NOT

The p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true. It is NOT the probability that the result occurred by chance. It is NOT a measure of practical significance or effect size. These are the three most common misinterpretations, and interviewers actively probe for them.

6.3 Type I and Type II Errors

H₀ is actually TRUEH₀ is actually FALSE
Reject H₀ Type I Error (α)
False Positive — we concluded there was an effect when there wasn't		 Correct (Power = 1−β)

  • α (Type I error rate) = The significance level. Set before the test. Typically 0.05.
  • β (Type II error rate) = The probability of failing to detect a real effect. Depends on sample size, effect size, and α.
  • Power (1−β) = The probability that the test correctly rejects a false H₀. Typically targeted at 0.80 (80%).

6.4 Common Statistical Tests

TestUse CaseAssumptions
One-sample t-test Test whether a sample mean differs from a known/hypothesized value Data is approx. normally distributed; σ unknown
Two-sample t-test (independent) Compare means of two independent groups Both groups approx. normal; equal or unequal variances (Welch's)
Paired t-test Compare means before and after a treatment on the same subjects Paired differences are approx. normally distributed

z-test Test whether a sample mean differs from μ when σ is known and n is large σ known; n ≥ 30
Worked Example — Two-Sample t-Test

A product team believes that redesigning a checkout button from grey to blue increases purchase completion rate. They run a test: 500 users see the grey button (control), 500 see the blue button (variant). Results: control mean completion = 68.2%, variant mean = 71.4%, pooled SE = 1.82%.

H₀: μ_blue = μ_grey (no difference in completion rate)

H₁: μ_blue ≠ μ_grey (two-tailed test)

Test statistic: t = (71.4 − 68.2) / 1.82 = 3.2 / 1.82 ≈ 1.76

p-value for t = 1.76, df ≈ 998: p ≈ 0.079

Decision (α = 0.05): p = 0.079 > 0.05 → Fail to reject H₀. The 3.2 percentage point difference is not statistically significant at the 5% level — we cannot conclude the blue button causes a real improvement with this sample size.

6.5 One-Tailed vs. Two-Tailed Tests

  • Two-tailed test: H₁ is "the parameter is different" (either direction). Reject if the test statistic is extreme in either tail. More conservative. Use when you have no prior reason to expect a direction.
  • One-tailed test: H₁ is "the parameter is larger (or smaller)." More statistical power in the specified direction but cannot detect effects in the opposite direction. Use only when there is a strong theoretical reason to test a specific direction.

7. Correlation

Definition

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables. It ranges from −1 (perfect negative linear relationship) through 0 (no linear relationship) to +1 (perfect positive linear relationship).

Fundamental Principle

Correlation does not imply causation. Two variables can be strongly correlated for many reasons: a causal relationship in either direction, a shared common cause (confounding variable), or pure chance (spurious correlation). Establishing causation requires experimental design (randomized controlled trials) or advanced causal inference methods.

7.1 Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r)

Measures the linear relationship between two continuous variables. Assumes that both variables are normally distributed and the relationship is linear.

Pearson r
r = Σ[(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)] / [√Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² × √Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²]
Equivalently: r = Cov(X,Y) / (σ_X × σ_Y). Ranges from −1 to +1.
|r| valueInterpretation
0.00 – 0.19Negligible / No correlation
0.20 – 0.39Weak correlation
0.40 – 0.59Moderate correlation
0.60 – 0.79Strong correlation
0.80 – 1.00Very strong correlation

7.2 Spearman Rank Correlation (ρ)

Measures the monotonic relationship between two variables by computing the Pearson correlation of the ranks of the values rather than the values themselves. Does not assume linearity or normality. Appropriate for ordinal data or when the relationship is monotonic but not linear.

Spearman ρ (simplified formula for no ties)
ρ = 1 − [6 × Σdᵢ²] / [n × (n² − 1)]
Where dᵢ = rank(xᵢ) − rank(yᵢ) is the difference in ranks for observation i.
Worked Example — Spearman Correlation

Five students are ranked on Math and English ability:

ρ = 1 − (6 × 4) / (5 × (25−1)) = 1 − 24/120 = 1 − 0.2 = 0.80

A strong positive monotonic relationship: students who rank highly in Math also tend to rank highly in English.

8. Regression Analysis

Regression analysis models the relationship between a dependent variable (the outcome we want to predict or explain) and one or more independent variables (predictors). It is the workhorse of both descriptive analysis and predictive modelling.

8.1 Simple Linear Regression

Model

ŷ = β₀ + β₁x + ε

Where ŷ is the predicted value of y, β₀ is the intercept (value of y when x=0), β₁ is the slope (change in y for a one-unit increase in x), and ε is the error term (residual).

Estimating Coefficients (OLS — Ordinary Least Squares)

OLS finds β₀ and β₁ that minimize the sum of squared residuals: SSR = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²

OLS Estimates
β₁ = Σ[(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ)] / Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² = Cov(X,Y) / Var(X)
β₀ = ȳ − β₁ × x̄

Goodness of Fit: R²

R² (Coefficient of Determination)

R² = 1 − (SSR / SST) = 1 − [Σ(yᵢ−ŷᵢ)²] / [Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)²]

R² measures the proportion of variance in y explained by x. R²=0.72 means the model explains 72% of the variance in y; the remaining 28% is unexplained (residual variance). R² ranges from 0 to 1 (and can be negative for very poor models). It does NOT indicate whether the model is correctly specified or whether coefficients are statistically significant.

Assumptions of Linear Regression (LINE)

  • L — Linearity: The relationship between x and y is linear.
  • I — Independence: Observations are independent of each other.
  • N — Normality: Residuals are normally distributed (important for inference, not for prediction).
  • E — Equal variance (Homoscedasticity): The variance of residuals is constant across all values of x. If it varies (heteroscedasticity), standard errors are biased.

8.2 Logistic Regression

Used when the dependent variable is binary (0/1, Yes/No, True/False). Instead of predicting the raw value, it predicts the probability that the outcome equals 1, using the sigmoid function to keep predictions in [0,1].

Logistic (Sigmoid) Function
P(y=1|x) = 1 / (1 + e^(−(β₀ + β₁x)))
The log-odds (logit): log[P/(1−P)] = β₀ + β₁x. A one-unit increase in x changes the log-odds by β₁. Interpreting the exponent: e^β₁ is the odds ratio — the multiplicative change in odds for a one-unit increase in x.
Worked Example — Logistic Regression

A model predicts customer churn based on number of support calls in the last 30 days. Fitted model: P(churn) = 1 / (1 + e^(−(−2.1 + 0.8 × calls)))

For a customer with 3 support calls:

Log-odds = −2.1 + 0.8 × 3 = −2.1 + 2.4 = 0.3

P(churn) = 1 / (1 + e^(−0.3)) = 1 / (1 + 0.741) = 1 / 1.741 ≈ 0.574 (57.4%)

This customer has a 57.4% predicted probability of churning. Using a 0.5 decision threshold, they would be flagged as likely to churn.

The odds ratio for "calls": e^0.8 ≈ 2.23. Each additional support call multiplies the odds of churn by 2.23.

9. Interview Questions

Statistics & Probability — Q&A
QWhat is the difference between variance and standard deviation? When would you use each?
Variance is the average squared deviation from the mean: s² = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²/(n−1). Its units are the square of the original units (e.g., ₹² for revenue data), which makes it hard to interpret directly.

Standard deviation is the square root of variance, returning to the original units (e.g., ₹). It is interpretable alongside the data — "average spread around the mean."

Use variance in mathematical derivations and proofs (it has nicer algebraic properties — variances of independent variables add directly, standard deviations do not). Use standard deviation when communicating to stakeholders or interpreting spread in context, as the units are meaningful.
QWhat is the p-value? What does it mean when p = 0.03?
The p-value is the probability of obtaining a test statistic as extreme as (or more extreme than) the one computed from the sample data, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

p = 0.03 means: if H₀ were true, there is only a 3% chance of observing data as extreme as what we observed. Since this is below the conventional threshold of α = 0.05, we reject H₀ and conclude the result is statistically significant at the 5% level.

What p = 0.03 does NOT mean: It does not mean H₀ has a 3% chance of being true. It does not mean the result is practically significant or large in magnitude. Statistical significance and practical significance are separate concepts — a tiny effect can be statistically significant with a large enough sample.
QWhat is the difference between Type I and Type II errors? Which is worse?
A Type I error (α) is a false positive: we reject H₀ when it is actually true. Example: concluding a new drug works when it has no real effect.

A Type II error (β) is a false negative: we fail to reject H₀ when it is actually false. Example: concluding a new drug has no effect when it actually does.

Which is worse depends entirely on context: In drug trials, a Type I error means approving an ineffective (or dangerous) drug — potentially very harmful; Type I errors are therefore controlled very tightly (α = 0.01 or 0.001). In cancer screening, a Type II error means missing a cancer case — the consequences of false negatives are severe, so tests are designed to minimize β even at the cost of more false positives (which are resolved by follow-up testing). There is always a tradeoff: reducing α (Type I) increases β (Type II) for a fixed sample size. The only way to reduce both simultaneously is to increase sample size.
QWhen would you use a t-test vs. a z-test?
Use a z-test when the population standard deviation (σ) is known and the sample size is large (n ≥ 30). In practice, σ is rarely known, so z-tests are most common in textbook examples or when working with proportions from large samples.

Use a t-test when σ is unknown (the typical case) and you are estimating it from the sample standard deviation (s). The t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal to account for the added uncertainty of estimating σ. As n increases, the t-distribution converges to the normal, so for large n the distinction becomes negligible. The t-test is the standard choice for most real-world mean comparisons.
QWhat is the Central Limit Theorem and why is it important?
The CLT states that the sampling distribution of the sample mean x̄ approaches a normal distribution with mean μ and standard error σ/√n as n increases, regardless of the shape of the underlying population distribution.

Its importance is profound: virtually all classical statistical inference (confidence intervals, t-tests, z-tests, regression) relies on normality assumptions — but not on the raw data being normally distributed. The CLT guarantees that sample statistics are approximately normally distributed for large samples, making these methods valid even when the underlying data is skewed, bimodal, or otherwise non-normal. Without the CLT, classical inference would be restricted to populations that are normally distributed, which would cover very few real-world phenomena.
QWhat is R² and what are its limitations?
R² (coefficient of determination) measures the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the model. R² = 0.82 means the model accounts for 82% of the variability in y; the remaining 18% is unexplained.

Limitations:
  • R² always increases (or stays the same) when predictors are added — even useless ones. This inflates R² in models with many predictors. Adjusted R² (which penalizes for number of predictors) is a fairer comparison.
  • A high R² does not mean the model is well-specified or that the assumptions are met.
  • A high R² does not establish causation.
  • R² can be misleading for non-linear relationships — a perfectly curved dataset can have R²=0 for a linear fit even though the relationship is deterministic.
  • For prediction tasks, out-of-sample metrics (cross-validated RMSE, MAE) are more relevant than in-sample R².
QExplain the concept of statistical power.
Statistical power is the probability that a test will correctly reject H₀ when H₀ is false — i.e., the probability of detecting a real effect when one exists. Power = 1 − β (where β is the Type II error rate).

Power is affected by four factors: (1) Sample size n — more data means more power (the most controllable factor); (2) Effect size — larger real effects are easier to detect; (3) Significance level α — a higher α gives more power but at the cost of more Type I errors; (4) Variability in the data — higher variance means lower power.

The standard target for power is 80% (0.80) — meaning if there is a real effect of the specified size, the test has an 80% chance of detecting it. In practice, a power analysis (conducted before the study) determines the required sample size to achieve target power.
QA normally distributed dataset has mean 100 and standard deviation 15. What percentage of values fall between 70 and 130?
Step 1: Compute Z-scores for both bounds.
Z for 70: z = (70−100)/15 = −2.0
Z for 130: z = (130−100)/15 = +2.0

Step 2: Look up probabilities.
P(Z ≤ 2.0) = 0.9772; P(Z ≤ −2.0) = 0.0228

Step 3: P(70 ≤ X ≤ 130) = 0.9772 − 0.0228 = 0.9544 ≈ 95.44%

This is the "2-sigma rule" — approximately 95% of a normal distribution falls within ±2 standard deviations of the mean (exact figure: 95.44%).
